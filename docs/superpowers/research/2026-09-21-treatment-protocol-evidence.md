# Treatment-to-Concern Mapping: Clinical Evidence, Safety and UK Compliance Review

*Research agent output, 2026-09-21. Companion to `2026-05-11-nad-iv-clinical-brief.md` (NAD+ is only updated here, not repeated).*

**Verification status.** Unlike the May brief, live web search was available. References marked **[V]** were retrieved and checked this session (search result or page fetch). References marked **[R]** are from recall (well-known guidelines/papers) and were not re-opened this session; they need a click-through check by the medical director before being quoted anywhere public. Nothing in this document is a fabricated citation; where no trial exists it says so.

**Bottom line in one paragraph.** None of the nine menu items has grade-A evidence for the quiz concerns they are currently mapped to. The only IV item with a positive placebo-controlled trial in healthy adults is high-dose IV vitamin C for short-term fatigue (one Korean RCT, effect measured to 24 h). The one placebo-controlled Myers' cocktail trial was null. IV NAD+ has no efficacy RCT at all, and the best new data (2024-2026) show that it is poorly tolerated and raises blood NAD+ *less* than IV NR. Methylene blue, EBOO and phospholipid exchange should not be recommended by an algorithm: methylene blue is a prescription-only medicine with a potentially fatal interaction with antidepressants, which is precisely the population that ticks "brain fog" and "mood swings". "Detox", "immunity", "anti-ageing" and "repairs cell membranes" claims have all been specifically ruled against by the ASA (three rulings, 20 December 2023). The authentic version of this product is: symptoms -> red-flag screen -> bloods -> lifestyle foundation -> *optional* clinic therapies carrying an honest evidence label, with the high-risk therapies reachable only through a doctor.

## 0. Grade table

## 1. Treatment-by-treatment review

### 1.1 NAD+ IV Drip — update only (2024-2026)

**New evidence since the May brief**

1. **Hawkins J, et al. (2024, medRxiv preprint, not peer reviewed).** "Randomized, placebo-controlled, pilot clinical study evaluating acute Niagen+ IV and NAD+ IV in healthy adults." Two phases, n=37 and n=16. 500 mg NR IV vs 500 mg NAD+ IV vs oral NR vs saline. NR IV infused ~75% faster with better tolerability; blood NAD+ at 3 h was significantly higher after NR IV than NAD+ IV; NAD+ IV raised white cell and neutrophil counts (possible inflammatory response), NR IV did not. Manufacturer-linked. https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.06.24308565v1 **[V]**
2. **Reyna et al. (2026), Frontiers in Aging.** Retrospective chart review in a commercial wellness chain, NAD+ n=6, NR n=8, 500 mg IV daily x4. **All six NAD+ clients reported moderate-to-severe abdominal cramping, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, raised heart rate, throat pain, congestion and chest pressure during infusion**; mean infusion 97 min vs 37 min for NR. No LFT/inflammatory changes at 30 days. Funded by Restore Hyperwellness, product donated by Niagen Bioscience; all authors company employees. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12907335/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1652582/full **[V]**
   - Both papers are commercially conflicted and tiny. But the conflict runs *against* IV NAD+, and they are the only comparative human data. Honest reading: the clinic's flagship molecule is the less efficient, less tolerable way of raising NAD+.
3. **Wu et al. (2025), eClinicalMedicine.** RCT of oral NR 2,000 mg/day in long COVID, n=58, 24 weeks, Mass General Brigham. NR raised NAD+ within 5 weeks. **No significant between-group differences in cognition, fatigue severity, or sleep quality.** Exploratory within-group improvements only. https://www.thelancet.com/journals/eclinm/article/PIIS2589-5370(25)00567-X/fulltext ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41357333/ **[V]** — This is the most directly relevant RCT to the quiz's fatigue / brain fog / sleep mapping, and it is null on its primary comparisons.
4. **NMN meta-analyses (2024-2025).** Pooled RCTs show NMN raises blood NAD+ but "most clinically relevant outcomes were not significantly different" from placebo; no significant benefit on fasting glucose, insulin, HbA1c, HOMA-IR or lipids (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39531138/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39116016/). Small signal on gait speed / physical performance in older adults (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39185644/ ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11365583/). **[V]**
5. **NOPARK (phase 3, NR 1 g/day, n=400 early Parkinson's, 52 weeks)** completed in 2025; results not published as of this search. https://cureparkinsons.org.uk/2026/01/2026-research-progress/ **[V]**. NR-SAFE (3 g/day, Nature Communications 2023) supports short-term oral safety. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-43514-6 **[V]**
6. **Product-quality safety signal (US).** FDA has reminded compounders that food-grade NAD+ is unsuitable for sterile compounding after adverse event reports (severe chills, rigors, vomiting, hypotension consistent with endotoxin), and there has been a Class I recall of NAD+ for injection for elevated endotoxin. https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/fda-reminds-compounders-use-ingredients-suitable-sterile-compounding ; https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/pln/news/fda-issues-class-i-recall-nad-injection-due-elevated-endotoxin-levels **[V]**. UK relevance: the clinic must be able to document a pharmaceutical-grade, MHRA "specials"-licensed or equivalent source with certificate of analysis including endotoxin.

**Evidence still missing:** no placebo-controlled RCT of IV NAD+ for fatigue, cognition, sleep, skin, DNA repair or ageing in humans. Grant et al. 2019 (n=11, PK only) remains the main IV paper.

**Verdict on current copy.** "Supports DNA repair and anti-aging at the molecular level" is unsupported in humans and is the exact category ("Anti-Ageing") the ASA upheld against in December 2023. Mapping to "poor sleep" and "cellular/skin" has no human data at all. Remove both.

**Safety/screening** as per the May brief (pregnancy/breastfeeding, active or recent cancer, severe renal/hepatic impairment, unstable cardiac disease, bipolar/psychosis). Add: infusion reactions are near-universal at commercial rates — consent must say so plainly.

### 1.2 IV Methylene Blue (methylthioninium chloride)

**Human evidence**

- **Rodriguez P, Zhou W, Barrett DW, et al. (2016), Radiology.** Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, n=26 healthy adults (22-62 y), **single oral dose** 280 mg. Increased insular/prefrontal fMRI activity; ~7% improvement in memory retrieval 1 h post dose. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27351678/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5084971/ **[V]**. Oral, single dose, surrogate-heavy, never replicated at scale.
- **Alda M, et al. (2017), British Journal of Psychiatry.** Randomised crossover, n=37, bipolar disorder on lamotrigine, oral 195 mg vs 15 mg for 6 months. Improved residual depression and anxiety scores; **no significant effect on cognitive function.** https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D3ACE2C595AADA7AF7410A8EF7BAF6D5/S0007125000280690a.pdf **[V]**. Note: the one cognition-relevant clinical trial was negative on cognition, and participants were on lamotrigine, not SSRIs.
- **HMTM / LMTM (TauRx) phase 3 Alzheimer's programme incl. LUCIDITY (NCT03446001).** Earlier phase 3 trials failed their primary endpoints; LUCIDITY reported favourable 12-month comparisons but the 24-month data do not show cognitive benefit on standard measures. Review of RCTs: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10631450/ ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12861207/ **[V]**. Not licensed for dementia anywhere.
- **No human RCT found** of intravenous methylene blue for brain fog, mood swings, "mitochondrial dysfunction" or cognitive enhancement in a wellness population.

**Grade: C for pharmacological plausibility; D for the mapped quiz use.**

**Safety — this is the most dangerous item on the menu for an automated quiz**

- **Serotonin syndrome.** Methylene blue is a potent reversible MAO-A inhibitor. FDA Drug Safety Communications (July 2011, updated 20 October 2011): serious and fatal CNS reactions when given with serotonergic psychiatric drugs; FDA advises it should generally not be given to patients on these drugs outside emergencies. Most cases involved SSRIs, SNRIs or clomipramine. https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/751978 ; https://www.apsf.org/article/methylene-blue-and-the-risk-of-serotonin-toxicity/ **[V]**. The UK SmPC (Proveblue) lists SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, bupropion, buspirone, clomipramine, mirtazapine, venlafaxine and opioids such as tramadol/pethidine as interacting. https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/6898/smpc **[V]**
  - Washout matters: fluoxetine needs ~5 weeks. "Are you on an antidepressant *now*" is not a sufficient question; ask "in the last 6 weeks".
  - Also screen: St John's wort, 5-HTP, tryptophan, triptans, lithium, linezolid, dextromethorphan, MDMA/recreational serotonergics, tramadol, fentanyl.
- **G6PD deficiency — contraindicated** (haemolytic anaemia; MB also does not work and can worsen methaemoglobinaemia). SmPC above **[V]**. G6PD deficiency is common in people of African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and South/South-East Asian ancestry — i.e. a substantial share of a London clientele. A lab G6PD assay before first dose is the defensible standard; a quiz question alone is inadequate because most carriers do not know.
- **Pregnancy — avoid** (fetal harm, intestinal atresia association, fetal hypoxia concerns); breastfeeding — avoid. **[V]**
- Severe renal impairment (dose accumulation), hypersensitivity to thiazine dyes; causes blue-green urine, interferes with pulse oximetry readings, dizziness, nausea, injection-site necrosis on extravasation.

**UK status.** Licensed **prescription-only medicine**, sole indication acute symptomatic treatment of drug/chemical-induced methaemoglobinaemia. Cognitive/mood use is **off-label** use of a POM. Consequences: (i) GMC prescribing standards for off-label use apply — individual assessment, sufficient evidence base, informed consent that it is off-label; (ii) **a POM must not be advertised to the public at all** (Human Medicines Regulations 2012 reg. 284; CAP Code rule 12.12). A quiz result page that names "IV Methylene Blue £395" and states a benefit is an advertisement for a POM. See section 2.

### 1.3 Immunity IV Drip (high-dose vitamin C / zinc)

**Human evidence**

- **Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013), Cochrane Database Syst Rev CD000980.** Regular *oral* vitamin C does **not** reduce cold incidence in the general population; reduces duration ~8% in adults, 14% in children; halves incidence only in people under heavy acute physical stress (marathon runners, soldiers). Therapeutic dosing after onset: no consistent effect. https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4/full **[V]**
- **Nault D, et al. (2024), Cochrane CD014914.** Oral zinc: little or no effect on preventing colds; may shorten an ongoing cold by ~2 days (low-certainty), more non-serious adverse effects. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38719213/ **[V]**
- **Suh SY, Bae WK, Ahn HY, et al. (2012), Nutrition Journal 11:7.** Double-blind RCT, n=141 healthy office workers, single 10 g IV vitamin C vs saline. Lower fatigue scores at 2 h and 1 day; effect larger in those with lower baseline vitamin C. https://doi.org/10.1186/1475-2891-11-7 **[V]**. This is a fatigue result, not an immunity result, and only to 24 h.
- **No RCT found** showing an IV vitamin C/zinc drip prevents infections or speeds recovery in healthy adults. The ASA's expert position is that such a claim requires RCTs vs placebo drip *and* vs oral supplementation (Get A Drip ruling).

**Grade: D for the IV product as mapped** (frequent colds, slow recovery). Oral vitamin C/zinc: B for modest shortening, not prevention.

**Safety**

- **G6PD deficiency:** haemolysis with high-dose vitamin C; meta-summary of 14 cases, 71% G6PD deficient, 57% IV, half with complications incl. AKI, DIC, methaemoglobinaemia. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8857720/ **[V]**. Standard practice is a G6PD assay before doses of roughly 15 g or more; many clinics test before any high-dose infusion.
- **Oxalate nephropathy / kidney stones / renal impairment:** Yanase et al. scoping review of IV high-dose vitamin C harms — oxalate nephropathy cases, hypernatraemia, **falsely elevated point-of-care glucometer readings** (dangerous for insulin users), haemolysis in G6PD deficiency. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32404636/ **[V]**
- Haemochromatosis / iron overload (vitamin C increases iron absorption and mobilisation) **[R]**; history of calcium oxalate stones; eGFR <60 warrants doctor review, <30 exclude.
- Zinc: nausea; chronic excess causes copper deficiency **[R]**.
- Pregnancy: no indication for high-dose IV; exclude.

### 1.4 Myers' Cocktail

**Human evidence**

- **Ali A, Njike VY, Northrup V, et al. (2009), J Altern Complement Med 15(3):247-57.** Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot, n=34, fibromyalgia, weekly infusion x8 vs lactated Ringer's. Both groups improved; **no statistically significant between-group difference on any outcome.** Strong placebo response. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19250003/ ; https://dx.doi.org/10.1089/acm.2008.0410 **[V]**
- **Gaby AR (2002), Altern Med Rev 7(5):389-403.** Narrative review and personal case experience; not controlled evidence. https://www.unboundmedicine.com/medline/citation/12410623/ **[V]**
- MSD Manual: "no data to support these claims" for energy, immunity or stress. https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/special-subjects/dietary-supplements/intravenous-vitamin-therapy-myers-cocktail **[V]**
- **ASA upheld** against "Classic Myers' (mood, energy, immunity)" by name — GMG Pharmacy ruling, 20 Dec 2023.

**Grade: D.** The honest sentence is: "In the only placebo-controlled trial, a saline-type drip worked as well as the vitamin drip."

**Safety.** Magnesium: hypotension, flushing, caution in renal impairment, heart block, myasthenia gravis. Rapid infusion syncope. Thiamine anaphylaxis (rare). Cannulation risks: phlebitis, infection, extravasation. Fluid load in heart failure. B6 neuropathy with repeated high dosing. Pregnancy: exclude from automated recommendation.

### 1.5 Detox IV Drip

- **No human RCT found** for any "detox" IV formulation on liver function, "toxin elimination", digestion, skin or weight in healthy people.
- **Klein AV, Kiat H (2015), J Hum Nutr Diet 28(6):675-86.** Critical review: no RCTs of commercial detox programmes; very little clinical evidence; studies that exist are methodologically flawed. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jhn.12286 **[V]**
- Physiologically, a person with a working liver and kidneys is not helped to "eliminate toxins" by IV vitamins or glutathione, and a person with *failing* liver or kidneys should not be in a wellness clinic.
- **ASA:** "Diet & Detox Detox/Fat Burning" (GMG Pharmacy) and "Detox Drip" (Reviv) upheld, 20 Dec 2023. CAP advice lists "cleanses vital organs" and "burn off stubborn fat" as problematic.

**Grade: D.** Mapping to digestive issues and weight struggles is the least defensible on the menu: persistent digestive symptoms are a recognised reason to test for coeliac disease, IBD and (with red flags) cancer; weight struggles have grade-A treatments elsewhere. At £399 it is also priced above every other single drip.

**Safety:** depends on contents (usually glutathione, vitamin C, B-complex, sometimes alpha-lipoic acid — ALA can cause hypoglycaemia, relevant to diabetics on insulin/sulfonylureas **[R]**). Inherits 1.3 and 1.6 screens.

### 1.6 Skin Glow IV (glutathione + vitamin C)

**Human evidence**

- **Zubair S, et al. (2016), J Pak Assoc Dermatol.** Placebo-controlled trial, n=32 analysed (16/arm), IV glutathione 1,200 mg twice weekly x6 weeks. 37.5% vs 18.7% reported lightening; by 6 months only 1/16 retained any effect. **32% had deranged liver function tests; one anaphylactic shock.** https://www.jpad.com.pk/index.php/jpad/article/view/18 **[V]**
- **Sonthalia S, et al. (2016/2018).** "Intravenous glutathione for skin lightening: inadequate safety data" (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27499402/) and "Glutathione for skin lightening: a regnant myth or evidence-based verity?" Dermatol Pract Concept (https://dpcj.org/index.php/dpc/article/view/dermatol-pract-concept-articleid-dp0801a04) **[V]**
- **Sarkar R, et al. (2025), Int J Dermatol** systematic review: IV glutathione not recommended — lack of efficacy and adverse effects. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ijd.17535 **[V]**. Narrative review 2025: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11862975/ **[V]**
- Hauser RA, et al. (2009), Mov Disord — IV glutathione in Parkinson's, n=21, no significant benefit (relevant only because it is the best-quality IV glutathione RCT in any indication). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19230029/ **[V]**
- **No RCT found** for "glow", radiance or "dullness" as an endpoint.

**Grade: D, with an adverse safety balance.**

**Regulatory / safety signals**

- **Philippines FDA Advisory 2019-182** ("Unsafe use of glutathione as skin lightening agent"): no published trials support injectable use; risks cited include liver, kidney and nervous system toxicity, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN, and infection transmission; theoretical long-term skin cancer concern from reduced melanin. https://www.fda.gov.ph/fda-advisory-no-2019-182-unsafe-use-of-glutathione-as-skin-lightening-agent/ **[V]**. The Philippine DOH repeated the warning in 2024 after reported deaths. https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1909337/doh-no-fda-approval-on-injectable-glutathione-for-skin-lightening **[V]**
- Not a licensed medicine in the UK for any indication.
- **Ethics:** in the UK market IV glutathione is substantially a skin-lightening product. A clinic that wants to be authentic should decide explicitly whether it is selling skin lightening. If not, it must not imply it ("brightening", "lightening", "even tone" all read that way). If the product stays, never target by ethnicity or skin tone.
- ASA upheld against "Gluta Glow (skin brightening)" by name (GMG Pharmacy, 20 Dec 2023).
- Screen: asthma (bronchospasm reports with glutathione, mainly inhaled **[R]**), sulphite sensitivity, liver disease, prior anaphylaxis, plus all vitamin C screens (1.3). Pregnancy: exclude.

### 1.7 EBOO (extracorporeal blood oxygenation and ozonation)

**Human evidence**

- **Di Paolo N, Bocci V, Salvo DP, et al. (2005), Int J Artif Organs 28(10):1039-50.** n=28 peripheral artery disease patients randomised to EBOO vs IV prostacyclin; EBOO better for ischaemic skin lesions; no change in arterial circulation. Single centre, unblinded, authors are the technique's inventors. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16288443/ ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/039139880502801012 **[V]**
- Di Paolo et al. (2005) descriptive series, >1,200 treatments in 82 patients. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16156950/ **[V]**
- **No human RCT found** for fatigue, slow recovery, digestive symptoms, "cellular detoxification" or "regeneration". The material visibly removed into the filter during EBOO, marketed as "toxins", has not been characterised in any peer-reviewed study I could find; it is plausibly lipid/protein/foam from ozonated plasma.

**Grade: D for every mapped concern.**

**Safety**

- This is a dialysis-type extracorporeal circuit: large-bore venous access (often two), systemic **heparin** anticoagulation, litres of blood through a filter. Risks are those of haemodialysis without the indication: bleeding, hypotension, haemolysis, air embolism, circuit clotting, blood-borne infection if consumables/reprocessing fail, vasovagal collapse.
- **Ozone-specific:** deaths from gas embolism reported with ozone autohaemotherapy (e.g. "An unexpected death during oxygen-ozone therapy"); German survey 1975-83 listing deaths, paraplegia, gas embolism, MI; 2024 case report of cerebral gas embolism with multifocal stroke during oxygen-ozone therapy (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11667332/); hepatitis C outbreak linked to ozone autohaemotherapy. **[V]** Most embolism deaths relate to direct IV gas or injection rather than closed-circuit EBOO, but the clinic should not rely on that distinction in consent.
- **Contraindications:** G6PD deficiency (oxidative haemolysis; listed by ozone societies themselves), pregnancy, anticoagulant therapy or bleeding disorder, thrombocytopenia, heparin allergy/HIT history, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, recent MI/stroke, severe anaemia, active bleeding, poor venous access, seizure disorder.

**Regulatory**

- **US FDA, 21 CFR 801.415:** "Ozone is a toxic gas with no known useful medical application in specific, adjunctive, or preventive therapy." No ozone device is FDA-cleared for blood treatment. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-801.415 **[R for URL; text V via secondary sources]**
- **UK:** I found **no MHRA statement specifically on ozone therapy or EBOO**. Ozone is not a licensed medicine. The ozone generator and extracorporeal circuit are medical devices and need valid UKCA/CE marking *for that intended purpose* — the owner should ask the supplier for the declaration of conformity and check whether blood ozonation is within the stated intended use. An extracorporeal blood procedure is unambiguously within CQC scope and would be expected in the statement of purpose.
- **ASA:** has ruled against a clinic's ozone therapy claims (COVID-19 treatment) — and CAP advice requires human trial evidence for objective therapy claims. https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/healthcare-medicinal-claims.html **[V]**

**Verdict.** A £1,995 invasive procedure with grade-D evidence must not be the output of a symptom quiz. If the clinic offers it at all, it is a doctor-initiated conversation with written consent that states the absence of evidence.

### 1.8 Phospholipid Exchange IV (phosphatidylcholine / "PK protocol")

- **No human RCT found** of IV phosphatidylcholine for brain fog, mood, cognition or "cell membrane repair". The "PK (Patricia Kane) protocol" literature is case reports and conference material. Searches of PubMed-indexed sources returned only (a) subcutaneous PC/deoxycholate for fat reduction, (b) oral polyenylphosphatidylcholine for liver disease, (c) reconstituted HDL (apoA-I/PC disc) infusion studies — none relevant to the claim.
- "Repairs cell membranes" is a mechanistic assertion with no clinical endpoint behind it. Infused phospholipid is handled as lipoprotein substrate; there is no human evidence it is selectively incorporated into neuronal membranes with functional benefit.

**Grade: D.**

**Safety.** IV PC products (Lipostabil/Essentiale N type) are solubilised with **deoxycholate** and preserved with **benzyl alcohol**; rapid infusion can cause haemolysis, phlebitis, hypotension, nausea and diarrhoea **[R]**. **Soy and egg allergy** must be screened. Not licensed in the UK or US; Brazil's regulator banned cosmetic Lipostabil injection for lack of evidence (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090820X03002115 **[V]**). Caution: antiphospholipid syndrome/coagulopathy, severe liver disease, hypertriglyceridaemia/pancreatitis history, pregnancy (benzyl alcohol; no data). Imported unlicensed product needs an MHRA-compliant importer (Guidance Note 14 route).

**Verdict.** Same as methylene blue: the people who tick "brain fog + mood swings" are disproportionately people with depression, perimenopause, thyroid disease or sleep disorders — all treatable, all missed if the algorithm sells a £425 drip.

### 1.9 Metabolic Health Programme (£1,500)

Contents were not specified, so this is graded conditionally.

- **Grade A components:** structured multicomponent lifestyle programmes (diet + activity + behavioural support) — NICE NG246 *Overweight and obesity management* (Jan 2025) **[R]**; total diet replacement for type 2 diabetes remission — DiRECT, Lean MEJ et al., Lancet 2018;391:541-51, n=306, 46% remission at 12 months **[R]**; GLP-1/GIP agonists — STEP-1 (Wilding, NEJM 2021) and SURMOUNT-1 (Jastreboff, NEJM 2022) **[R]**.
- **Grade D components:** any drip, "metabolic reset", or "detox" element.
- **Digestive mapping:** not defensible. A weight programme is not a treatment for digestive symptoms.
- **Advertising trap:** if the programme includes GLP-1 medicines, they are POMs. The ASA published nine rulings in July 2025 and further rulings in December 2025 under rule 12.12; "weight loss injections", "weight loss pen", "GLP-1" and similar indirect references count as advertising a POM. https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/weight-control-prescription-only-medicines.html ; https://www.asa.org.uk/news/how-we-re-trimming-down-problem-ads-for-weight-loss-prescription-only-medicines.html ; GPhC notice: https://www.pharmacyregulation.org/about-us/news-and-updates/updated-enforcement-notice-issued-weight-management-prescription-medicine-ads **[V]**. The quiz may say "a doctor-led weight management consultation"; it may not say or imply the drug.
- Screen: pregnancy/trying to conceive, eating disorder history (essential for any weight pathway), pancreatitis, medullary thyroid cancer/MEN2, gallbladder disease, insulin/sulfonylurea use, BMI.

## 2. UK regulatory position (applies to the whole menu)

**CQC.** IV administration of vitamins/nutrients and of POMs (including 0.9% saline, which is a POM) is treated by CQC as the regulated activity "Treatment of disease, disorder or injury" and requires registration; there is no "wellness" exemption. CQC scope page: https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/registration/scope-registration/regulated-activities/treatment-disease-disorder-or-injury (page blocked automated fetch; position confirmed via secondary sources incl. https://astoriacc.co.uk/do-iv-drip-clinics-need-cqc-registration-in-england/) **[V, secondary]**. EBOO must be in the statement of purpose.

**Medicines law.** Under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, medicinal products for parenteral administration are prescription-only (reg. 62 and Sch. 1 criteria) **[R]**. Every drip therefore needs a patient-specific prescription or Patient Specific Direction from an appropriate prescriber after an assessment. PGDs cannot be used for unlicensed medicines **[R]**. NAD+, glutathione and IV phosphatidylcholine are **unlicensed** in the UK; they can only be supplied as "specials" to meet the special clinical need of an individual patient — MHRA Guidance Note 14: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf **[V]**. GN14 also states that **unlicensed medicines must not be advertised** **[R]**. Methylene blue is licensed but used off-label.

**GMC.** *Good practice in proposing, prescribing, providing and managing medicines and devices*: prescribe unlicensed medicines only where, after assessing the individual, it is necessary for medical reasons; be satisfied there is sufficient evidence or experience of safety and efficacy; take responsibility for follow-up; tell the patient it is unlicensed. https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-practice-in-prescribing-and-managing-medicines-and-devices/prescribing-unlicensed-medicines **[V]**. A quiz that selects the unlicensed medicine *before* the doctor has seen the patient inverts this. Remote prescribing of injectables without adequate assessment is a recurring fitness-to-practise theme **[R]**.

**GPhC.** Guidance for registered pharmacies preparing unlicensed medicines: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf **[V]** — relevant to whoever compounds the bags.

**Two points the owner may not have clocked**

1. **Drip names are claims.** "Immunity IV Drip", "Detox IV Drip" and "Skin Glow IV" are, as names, the claims the ASA upheld against. Rewording the description underneath does not fix a product name.
2. **A quiz result is an advertisement.** Output on the clinic's own website recommending a priced product is marketing within the CAP Code's remit. For methylene blue (a POM), and arguably for any product containing a POM, naming it to a member of the public is a breach of rule 12.12 and HMR reg. 284 regardless of how careful the wording is. I did not find an ASA ruling specifically about hangover drips in this search; "hangover cure" would nonetheless fall under rules 3.7/12.1 and the alcohol-harm social responsibility rules. I also found no ASA ruling that names NAD+ specifically.

References in this section are standard UK guidance **[R]**: NICE NG206 (ME/CFS, 2021), NICE CKS Tiredness/fatigue in adults, NICE CKS Insomnia, NICE NG222 (Depression, 2022), NICE NG23 (Menopause, updated 2024), NICE NG20 (Coeliac), NICE NG12 (Suspected cancer), NICE NG246 (Obesity, 2025), NICE CG61/BSG IBS guidance, NICE NG226 (Osteoarthritis, 2022), NICE NG100 (Rheumatoid arthritis), NICE NG202 (Obstructive sleep apnoea), NICE NG28 (Type 2 diabetes).

|---|---|---|---|
| **Brain fog** | Treat the cause. Sleep regularity; aerobic exercise (RCT-level evidence for cognition); alcohol reduction; medication review (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids); HRT where perimenopausal; treat depression/anxiety | Hypothyroidism, B12/folate deficiency, iron deficiency, perimenopause, depression, sleep apnoea, diabetes, coeliac, long COVID, ADHD, medication effects, alcohol | Progressive memory loss, new focal neurology, new severe headache, confusion, personality change, seizure |

## 4. Conclusions

### (a) Mapping audit: defensible / reword / remove

| Current mapping | Verdict | Action |
|---|---|---|
| NAD+ -> low energy, slow recovery | **Reword.** Keep as an *optional, grade-C, experiential* item after bloods and consultation | Remove "chronic fatigue" as a symptom label (reads as a medical condition; ME/CFS patients may react badly to infusions and need NG206 care) |
| NAD+ -> poor sleep | **Remove** | No evidence; CBT-I is the answer |
| NAD+ -> "cellular/skin"; "supports DNA repair and anti-aging at the molecular level" | **Remove** | Unsupported in humans; ASA "anti-ageing" precedent |
| NAD+ -> cognitive | **Reword/soften** | The best RCT of NAD+ repletion for cognition and fatigue (Wu 2025) was null |
| Methylene blue -> brain fog, mood swings, cognitive | **Remove from automated recommendation entirely** | POM: cannot be advertised. Serotonin syndrome risk is concentrated in exactly the people this mapping selects. Doctor-initiated only, after medication reconciliation and G6PD assay |
| Immunity IV -> frequent colds, slow recovery | **Remove mapping; rename product** | Cochrane evidence is against prevention; ASA upheld against "Immunity" drips. If retained on the menu, rename (e.g. "Vitamin C & Zinc Infusion") and do not map to infection |
| Myers' -> fatigue, general wellness | **Reword.** Only honest framing: hydration plus vitamins, experiential, no proven advantage over placebo drip or oral supplements | Do not map to "fatigue" as if therapeutic. One defensible route: offer **if bloods show a deficiency** and the patient prefers parenteral repletion |
| Detox IV -> digestive, skin dullness, weight | **Remove all three mappings; rename or retire the product** | No evidence, no mechanism, ASA precedent, and the mappings divert people from coeliac/IBD/cancer work-up |
| Skin Glow -> skin dullness | **Remove from automated recommendation; rename** | Negative benefit-risk in the only IV trial; Philippine FDA warning; skin-lightening ethics. Replace with evidence-based skin pathway (SPF, retinoid, clinic dermatology/aesthetics services) |
| EBOO -> fatigue, slow recovery, digestive | **Remove entirely from quiz** | Grade D, invasive, £1,995. Doctor-led only, if offered at all. Owner should seriously consider whether an "authentic" brand wants this product |
| Phospholipid Exchange -> brain fog, mood swings | **Remove entirely from quiz** | Grade D, unlicensed import, no trials; mapped population needs mental health / endocrine assessment |
| Metabolic Programme -> weight | **Defensible (the only one)** | Provided the programme is NICE-aligned, screens for eating disorders, and does not reference POMs |
| Metabolic Programme -> digestive | **Remove** | Not a digestive treatment |

**Net result:** one fully defensible mapping (metabolic programme -> weight), two retained with honest rewording and consultation gating (NAD+ -> energy/recovery; Myers'/vitamin C -> short-term fatigue or documented deficiency), everything else leaves the algorithm. The clinic's credibility gain from a quiz that frequently says "get these bloods first" is, commercially, the point: a £150-£350 blood panel plus doctor consultation is a saleable, defensible, CQC-friendly first product, and it legitimately generates downstream treatment.

### (b) Honest protocol structure per concern

Every result page should have the same four layers, in this order, with layer 3 visually dominant:

1. **Safety gate** — red-flag answer -> "Please see your GP / call 111 / 999" and *no* product shown.
2. **Find the cause (diagnostics first).**
   - **Core panel (all concerns):** FBC, ferritin (+ transferrin saturation), B12 (active B12 if available) and folate, 25-OH vitamin D, TSH (reflex fT4), HbA1c, U&E with eGFR, LFTs, CRP, lipids.
   - **Add-ons by concern:**
     - Fatigue / brain fog: coeliac tTG-IgA + total IgA; morning cortisol only if clinical features; FSH/oestradiol are *not* required to diagnose perimenopause over 45 (NICE NG23 — clinical diagnosis); testosterone (men, 9 am) if other features; STOP-Bang questionnaire; PHQ-9/GAD-7.
     - Poor sleep: ferritin (restless legs), TSH, STOP-Bang -> home sleep study referral; Insomnia Severity Index.
     - Frequent illness: FBC differential, immunoglobulins (IgG/A/M), HbA1c, HIV (offer routinely — London is a high-prevalence area), vitamin D.
     - Skin: ferritin, TSH, B12; androgens if acne/hirsutism.
     - Digestive: coeliac serology, faecal calprotectin, FIT where age/symptoms meet NG12, H. pylori stool antigen, CA-125 (women 50+ with bloating).
     - Weight: HbA1c, lipids, LFTs, TSH; PCOS panel where indicated; BP, waist; SCOFF eating-disorder screen.
     - Mood: TSH, B12, folate, vitamin D, FBC; PHQ-9, GAD-7, Mood Disorder Questionnaire; cycle tracking for PMDD.
     - Slow recovery: ferritin, vitamin D, CK, TSH, testosterone (men), energy-availability history.
     - Joint pain: CRP, ESR, RF, anti-CCP, urate, vitamin D.
     - **Before any high-dose vitamin C, methylene blue or ozone: G6PD assay. Before any infusion: eGFR and a pregnancy test where applicable.**
3. **Foundation (evidence grade A-B)** — the 3-4 lifestyle/licensed interventions from section 3 for that concern, stated concretely (e.g. "CBT-I: 6-8 weeks, 70-80% respond" rather than "improve sleep hygiene").
4. **Clinic therapies (optional)** — each card shows: name, price, **evidence grade badge with a one-sentence plain-English reason**, "requires doctor consultation", unlicensed/off-label status, and common side effects. Example badge text:
   - NAD+ IV — *Evidence: C. Raises blood NAD+. No clinical trial has yet shown it improves energy or cognition. Infusion commonly causes chest tightness, nausea and cramping, which settle on slowing the drip. Unlicensed in the UK; prescribed individually by our doctors.*
   - Vitamin infusion — *Evidence: C-D. One trial found IV vitamin C reduced fatigue for about a day in office workers. In the only placebo-controlled trial of a Myers'-type drip, a plain fluid drip did just as well.*
   - Methylene blue, EBOO, phospholipid exchange, glutathione — **not shown.** At most a neutral line: "Our doctors offer further therapies, discussed only in consultation where appropriate."

   Rule for the scoring engine: a clinic therapy can never be the top-ranked item, can never be shown when a red flag or hard contraindication is present, and can never be shown without layers 2 and 3.

### (c) Hard contraindication questions the quiz must ask

Ask these before any therapy card renders. "Yes" / "not sure" suppresses the listed items and adds a "discuss with our doctor" note. These are screening prompts, not a substitute for the prescriber's assessment.

1. **Are you pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding?** -> suppress *all* therapies.
2. **Are you under 18?** -> suppress all.
3. **In the last 6 weeks have you taken any antidepressant or any of:** SSRIs (sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, paroxetine), SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), mirtazapine, trazodone, tricyclics (amitriptyline, clomipramine), MAOIs, bupropion, buspirone, lithium, tramadol / pethidine / fentanyl, triptans, linezolid, St John's wort, 5-HTP or tryptophan, ADHD stimulants, MDMA? -> **hard block methylene blue.**
4. **Have you ever been told you have G6PD deficiency or favism, had a reaction to fava beans, antimalarials or sulfa drugs, or has a blood relative?** Plus: "Have you had a G6PD blood test?" -> block methylene blue, high-dose vitamin C (Immunity, Detox, Skin Glow), EBOO until assay result.
5. **Kidney disease, reduced kidney function, a single kidney, or a history of kidney stones?** -> block high-dose vitamin C; doctor review for magnesium-containing drips, methylene blue, NAD+.
6. **Haemochromatosis or iron overload?** -> block vitamin C drips.
7. **Blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, heparin, clopidogrel/ticagrelor), a bleeding or clotting disorder, low platelets, or previous reaction to heparin?** -> block EBOO; doctor review for all cannulation.
8. **Cancer now or treated in the last 5 years, or currently on chemo-/immuno-/radiotherapy?** -> block NAD+, high-dose antioxidants (vitamin C, glutathione — may interfere with treatment), all others to doctor.
9. **Heart failure, heart attack or stroke in the last 6 months, unstable angina, arrhythmia, uncontrolled blood pressure?** -> block NAD+, EBOO, magnesium drips; doctor review.
10. **Liver disease or abnormal liver tests?** -> block glutathione, phospholipid IV, NAD+; doctor review.
11. **Diabetes treated with insulin or a sulfonylurea?** -> flag: high-dose vitamin C falsifies finger-prick glucose readings; alpha-lipoic acid hypoglycaemia; GLP-1 programme needs dose coordination.
12. **Allergy to soy, egg, sulphites, thiamine/B vitamins, benzyl alcohol, or any previous reaction to an infusion? Asthma?** -> block phospholipid IV (soy/egg), doctor review glutathione (asthma/sulphite).
13. **Bipolar disorder, psychosis, or current thoughts of self-harm?** -> no therapy cards; mental health signposting. (NAD+ and MB relative contraindication; more importantly the wrong pathway.)
14. **Epilepsy / seizures; overactive thyroid; severe anaemia; myasthenia gravis?** -> block EBOO (first three); block magnesium drips (myasthenia).
15. **Current or past eating disorder?** -> block metabolic/weight programme auto-recommendation and any "detox"/weight framing.
16. **Pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, personal/family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2?** -> flag for metabolic programme if it involves GLP-1s.
17. **Red-flag symptom screen** (unintentional weight loss, night sweats, blood in stool/urine/sputum, difficulty swallowing, chest pain on exertion, new severe headaches, lumps, swollen hot joint, morning joint stiffness >30 min, memory loss, witnessed apnoeas) -> GP message, no products.

Data note: these answers are special category health data under UK GDPR. The repo's webhook currently flattens quiz payloads to a CRM; contraindication answers should need explicit consent, a lawful basis under Art. 9, and should not be sent to advertising pixels (Meta Pixel/CAPI are present in this codebase — health answers must never be passed as event parameters).

### (d) ASA-safe wording

General rules: no efficacy claim you cannot support with RCTs of the *IV product* in *healthy adults* vs placebo drip and oral supplementation (Get A Drip standard) — which, for this menu, means essentially no efficacy claims. Describe **what it is, who prescribes it, what is and is not known**. Do not name conditions. Do not use "detox", "immunity/immune boost", "anti-ageing", "cleanse", "repair", "restore", "regenerate", "fat-burning", "glow/brightening" as benefit claims or in product names. Do not name or indirectly reference POMs. Always state consultation-led and, where true, unlicensed.

| Treatment | Replace | With (suggested) |
|---|---|---|
| NAD+ IV | "supports DNA repair and anti-aging at the molecular level" | "An infusion of NAD+, a coenzyme involved in cellular energy metabolism. IV NAD+ raises blood NAD+ levels; clinical trials have not yet shown whether this improves energy, cognition or ageing outcomes. Unlicensed in the UK and offered only after consultation with one of our doctors. Most people feel chest tightness, nausea or flushing during the infusion, which ease when the drip is slowed." |
| IV Methylene Blue | "targets mitochondrial dysfunction to restore cognitive clarity" | **No public wording. Do not list, price or describe** (POM, rule 12.12 / HMR reg. 284). Internal/doctor-facing material only. |
| Immunity IV | name + "immunity" claims | Rename "Vitamin C & Zinc Infusion". "An infusion of vitamin C and zinc, prescribed after a medical consultation. Research on vitamin C and zinc taken by mouth shows a small reduction in how long colds last, not in how often they occur; drips have not been shown to work better than tablets. A G6PD blood test and kidney function check are required first." |
| Myers' Cocktail | "fatigue, general wellness" | "A vitamin and mineral infusion (magnesium, B vitamins, vitamin C) in fluid. In the one placebo-controlled trial, it performed no better than a plain fluid drip. Some clients choose it as part of a consultation-led plan, particularly where blood tests show a deficiency." |
| Detox IV | everything | Retire the name. If the formulation is kept: "[Formulation name] — an infusion of [ingredients]. We make no claims that it removes toxins; your liver and kidneys do that." Better: fold into the vitamin infusion and delete the SKU. |
| Skin Glow IV | name + glutathione claims | Recommend withdrawal from public menu. If kept: "Glutathione and vitamin C infusion. There is no good evidence that it improves skin appearance, and trials of IV glutathione have reported liver test abnormalities and allergic reactions. Not offered for skin lightening." (It is doubtful anyone would buy this, which is itself the answer.) |
| EBOO | "comprehensive cellular detoxification and regeneration" | No quiz wording. If listed on the site at all: "An extracorporeal procedure in which blood is passed through a filter circuit and exposed to an oxygen-ozone mixture, using a blood thinner. It has not been shown in clinical trials to treat fatigue or any other condition, and ozone therapy is not recognised as effective by medicines regulators. Available only after a doctor's assessment, blood tests and detailed consent." |
| Phospholipid Exchange IV | "repairs cell membranes" | No quiz wording. If listed: "An infusion of phosphatidylcholine, an unlicensed imported medicine. There are no clinical trials showing benefit for cognition or mood. Discussed only in consultation with our doctors." |
| Metabolic Health Programme | "weight, digestive" | "A doctor-led weight and metabolic health programme: baseline blood tests and body composition, a personalised nutrition and activity plan, and regular clinical review. Any prescribed treatment is decided by the doctor following assessment." No reference to injections, pens, GLP-1 or drug names. |

Site-wide additions: named GMC-registered medical director; CQC provider ID; a public "How we grade evidence" page explaining A-D (this is the authenticity asset, and it is also the best defence if a complaint reaches the ASA); testimonials must not reference conditions or imply efficacy; prices shown for consultations and blood panels are unproblematic, prices for POMs shown to the public are not.

## 5. Caveats on this review

- This was a rapid evidence review (one session, English-language, open-access sources), not a systematic review. Absence statements mean "not found in targeted PubMed/Cochrane/web searches", not proven non-existence.
- CQC's scope page and PubMed abstract pages blocked automated fetching; the CQC position is cited via its URL plus concordant secondary sources, and should be confirmed directly.
- No MHRA or GMC statement specific to ozone/EBOO, and no ASA ruling naming NAD+ or hangover drips, was found. That is a gap in the public record, not a green light.
- [R]-tagged items (NICE guideline numbers, HMR regulation numbers, DiRECT/STEP/SURMOUNT, Martineau, Prather, Morton, 21 CFR 801.415 URL, excipient details for IV phosphatidylcholine) are from recall and need click-through verification before external use.
- The clinic's medical director carries the prescribing responsibility and must sign off the contraindication logic; this document is decision support, not a clinical protocol.
